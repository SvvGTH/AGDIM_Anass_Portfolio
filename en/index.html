The problem

Every sheep and goat wears an ear tag with an 8-digit number on two lines. Agents copy it by hand into their phone, often in areas with no network. Typing mistakes end up in a database of 2.7 million records.

The photos are small (375 × 500 pixels on median), taken in harsh sunlight, of dirty or faded tags held at every angle.

Why 0.74 and not 0.90

0.90 is the target set at kick-off, before anyone had looked at the photos: 90 tags out of 100 read with no error at all. 0.74 is what the delivered model reaches on the test set: 74 tags out of 100. The 16-point gap comes from three things:

  • The images, not the model size. The large model (TrOCR-base) only gets to 0.78. A heavier model does not close the gap, and it would not fit in the phone’s 50 MB budget anyway.
  • Errors you cannot screen out in advance. Four errors out of five come from sharp photos. Filtering on sharpness is not enough to spot the tags that will be misread.
  • What it means in the field. When it is unsure, the reader hands control back to the agent instead of writing a number. Much of the gap therefore turns into manual entries, not wrong numbers in the database.

What I built

  1. DataExtracted the images and the typed numbers from PostgreSQL and Azure Blob Storage, then cleaned them.
  2. ModelFine-tuned TrOCR with PyTorch, with the vocabulary cut down to the digits 0 to 9 so a 0 can never become an O.
  3. OrientationA small classifier straightens the photo before reading.
  4. CompressionONNX export, vocabulary pruning and INT8 quantization to fit in 30 MB.
  5. IntegrationReading module inside the company’s React Native app, model deployed via Azure. The tool is in production with the 450 agents.

The decisions that mattered

  • The small model over the large one. TrOCR-base read 6 points better than TrOCR-small, but weighed 230 to 280 MB once quantized. I kept the small one (30 MB) to stay under the 50 MB limit.
  • No horizontal flips during data augmentation, so the model never learns to reverse the digit order.
  • Write only when the reader is sure. A doubtful reading goes back to the agent instead of being saved, because a wrong number in the database costs more than a manual entry.

What only the field revealed

  • Thresholds that filtered nothing. The app compared the model’s confidence to thresholds of 0.3, 0.5 and 0.85. Our confidences range from 0.89 to 0.99, so a wrong reading passed with two green ticks. I replaced those thresholds with a criterion built for our reader, covered by 169 tests.
  • The upside-down tag. Read at 180°, a tag gives the two lines swapped, with the same confidence as the right reading. The first digit follows the national numbering plan: by taking its real frequency into account, the reader picks the right order.
  • The final test on an Android phone: four tags in two colours, two held upside down and one turned 90°, all read correctly.

Limits and next steps

  • A single wrong digit can still pass every confidence check: that is the main remaining risk.
  • Next: a lightweight detector (YOLO nano type) to isolate the tag before reading, more labelled images, and trying more recent OCR models.

Making the code reliable

  • Nine different metric calculations merged into a single module, with an equality check on 40,000 real rows.
  • A privacy flaw fixed: an undeclared INTERNET permission, inherited from a third-party library and found in the Android logs.
  • A slow test suite fixed, CI widened, and the project’s invariants written down.

Two of us, with Melek Chaari
Data
35,918 Lending Club loans (2007 to 2011), 6,617 applications to score
Result
2nd on the Kaggle public leaderboard at submission

Python, pandas, scikit-learn, LightGBM, CatBoost, TF-IDF, Matplotlib, Jupyter.

The problem

The LendSmart fund has to decide, for each loan application, whether to fund it. Our model gives a probability of default; the investment committee turns it into a yes or a no.

The two mistakes do not cost the same. Funding a loan that defaults loses about 43 % of the amount; turning down a loan that would have been repaid only gives up about 19 %. A default therefore costs 2.3 times more than a good loan turned down.

The trap: columns that already know the ending

Some columns only exist after the loan is granted: repayments, recoveries, date of last payment. The recoveries column alone reaches an AUC of 0.994, while the full model tops out around 0.71. That is the alarm bell.

  • 11 columns removed, found from the data: 100 % empty in the applications to score, or constant. The notebook stops if that list ever stops matching.
  • Two hidden leaks: the instalment was computed on the amount finally funded, so we recomputed it on the amount requested; the grade duplicated the sub-grade.
  • Blanks that mean “never”: a borrower with no known delinquency has no date of last delinquency. Instead of a misleading median, a “never” flag and a value older than any observed.

Validate the way it will be used

Every application to score comes after the known loans, and the default rate moved: 26 % in 2007, around 14 % from 2009. So we train on every loan up to a date and validate on the next quarter, three times (Q1, Q2 and Q3 2011). A random split would flatter the model, since it would learn from 2011 to predict 2010. Every choice is made on this validation, never on the Kaggle leaderboard.

The model, in four steps

The interest rate alone ranks loans with an AUC of 0.668. Rule set before starting: an idea is kept only if the mean AUC goes up and no quarter goes down.

  1. Baseline, 0.7085Logistic regression and gradient boosting on 29 features, including loan-to-income ratios: $10,000 weighs heavily on a $30,000 income, lightly on $150,000.
  2. The borrower’s own words, 0.7101Job title and loan title in TF-IDF: “university” or “medical center” lower the risk, “bills” or “tax” raise it.
  3. Separate texts and LightGBM, 0.71393- to 5-letter pieces to catch typos, 2007 and 2008 loans counted at half weight, LightGBM instead of plain gradient boosting.
  4. CatBoost as a third voice, 0.7160CatBoost reads each whole job title as a category. Final blend: 37.5 % logistic, 37.5 % LightGBM, 25 % CatBoost.

The public Kaggle score went up at every step, from 0.7035 to 0.7098. About thirty other ideas were rejected by the same rule.

From score to decision

  • Choose and judge on different quarters. The threshold is chosen on Q1 and Q2 and judged on Q3: choosing and judging on the same quarter would be like picking lottery numbers after the draw.
  • The break-even threshold is not enough. It assumes every loan earns the same, while a repaid 60-month loan earns twice as much as a 36-month one. High-risk loans often pay for their losses through their rate.
  • Our advice to the committee: fund when the predicted risk is below about 50 %. Between 45 and 51 %, profit moves by barely 0.7 %: a more precise number would be noise.
  • Probabilities you can trust. In each tenth of loans sorted by risk, predicted and observed default stay within 3 points.

Fairness and limits

  • Zip code is removed, because it points to a neighbourhood. Rejection rates stay low whatever the home ownership status (0.5 to 1.4 %).
  • The gain over “fund everything” is modest (+0.3 to +1.1 % of profit). The model’s real value: avoiding bad rules and giving probabilities that can be used to price loans.
  • The applications to score are larger and longer than the known loans: the model should be retrained and its probabilities checked every quarter.
